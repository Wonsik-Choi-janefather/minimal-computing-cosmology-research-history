# Chapter 21 Needs, Rewards, Goals, and the Direction of Action

### 1 Scene and question

Understanding action requires examining sought and avoided outcomes and personally important goals. The same activity may involve different experiences and choices when undertaken from interest or for external evaluation. Motivation is not fully measured by the amount of behavior. (Spielman et al., 2020, §10.1 [R1](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/10-1-motivation))

Chapter question: How do goals, expectations, and costs change action initiation and persistence?

### 2 Starting points in established research

#### 2.1 Distinguishing needs, goals, and action

Hunger, a goal of passing an examination, and actually starting to study are related but distinct. Goals may conflict, or costs may prevent action despite a goal. (Spielman et al., 2020, §10.1 [R1](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/10-1-motivation))

#### 2.2 Expectations and costs

Ajzen's theory of planned behavior distinguishes attitudes, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, intentions, and behavior. It is not read here as one theory of every effort cost. (Ajzen, 1991 [R47](https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-5978(91)90020-T))

#### 2.3 External rewards and intrinsic motivation

Deci and colleagues' meta-analysis examined relationships with intrinsic motivation by reward type and delivery conditions. Its findings cannot be reduced to all external rewards always increasing or decreasing motivation. (Deci et al., 1999 [R54](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627))

### 4 WRRA-specific transformation

Transformation specification: connect goals, residue, and costs to action priorities.

Goals enter WRRA as conditions setting action priorities. Goal-directed expectations, residues of earlier outcomes, current costs, and available actions are distinguished. Reward responses, actual enjoyment, and willingness to persist are not combined into one variable.

#### 4.1 Intrinsic reasons and external rewards

Activities sustained by interest and meaning may differ experientially from those seeking external evaluation, but these motives are not always exclusive. Reward form, delivery, and choice must be investigated together. WRRA examines personal goals rather than computing all motivation from external reward magnitude alone.

#### 4.2 Distinguishing needs, goals, and action — WRRA interpretation

WRRA separates goal presence from execution conditions, recording what was sought and which inputs and resources were actually used.

#### 4.3 Expectations and costs — WRRA interpretation

Expectations, effort, time, and failure burdens are candidate variables with defined units and measures. Connections from experiential residue to expectation and from current conditions to execution are hypotheses for this book to test.

### 5 Outputs and execution record

The current output is an explanatory specification of phenomena, variables, and validation paths. Planned outputs: changes in initiation, persistence, and choice.

### 6 Falsification conditions and interpretive limits

Falsification condition: redefine the objective term if assumed goals do not explain the person's choices.

Researchers must establish participants' goals. Maximizing the researcher's score is not necessarily their sole purpose. Minimal computation is assessed under explicit goals and maintenance conditions; optimization ignoring other goals is limited accordingly.

#### 6.1 Interpreting persistence and stopping

Long persistence does not establish good goal adaptation. Stopping an activity whose value has fallen or cost risen may also be rational. Duration, outcomes, and alternative values are considered together. Minimal computation evaluates reduced unnecessary expenditure under goal-achievement and maintenance criteria.
