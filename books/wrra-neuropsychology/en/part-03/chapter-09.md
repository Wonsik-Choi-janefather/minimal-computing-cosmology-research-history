# Chapter 9 Sensation

### 1 Scene and question

Sensation begins the processing of physical events such as light and sound by the nervous system. A stimulus's presence must be distinguished from a report of detecting it. Reporting a faint sound may involve confidence and response criteria as well as sensory sensitivity. (Spielman et al., 2020, §5.1 [R1](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/5-1-sensation-versus-perception))

Chapter question: How can sensory detection be separated from differences in response criteria?

### 2 Starting points in established research

#### 2.1 Sensory transduction and modalities

Sensory reception connects a stimulus's physical properties to nervous-system activity. Light, sound, and mechanical pressure involve different input types and receptors, so even the phrase stimulus intensity requires modality-specific units. (Spielman et al., 2020, §5.1 [R1](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/5-1-sensation-versus-perception))

#### 2.2 Thresholds and adaptation

Detection thresholds are estimated under specified response criteria and procedures. Responses near a threshold can vary across trials, so one correct response does not establish a threshold. Adaptation under sustained stimulation is investigated separately from changes in input. Equating reported desensitization with receptor changes requires further evidence about transmission. (Spielman et al., 2020, §5.1 [R1](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/5-1-sensation-versus-perception))

#### 2.3 Four response outcomes

Reporting a present signal is a hit; reporting its absence is a miss. Reporting a signal when none is present is a false alarm; reporting absence correctly is a correct rejection. An increase in hit rate alone may reflect a strategy of always reporting presence. All four outcomes help distinguish sensitivity from criterion. (Spielman et al., 2020, §5.1 [R1](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/5-1-sensation-versus-perception))

### 4 WRRA-specific transformation

Transformation specification: separate sensory sensitivity, response criteria, and effects of previous trials.

WRRA distinguishes external stimuli, transmission, present state, and response rules. If detection rates change for the same stimulus, sensory transmission or the decision criterion for avoiding misses may have changed. Without separating them, input changes and interpretive changes can be confused.

#### 4.1 Sensory transduction and modalities — WRRA interpretation

### 6 Falsification conditions and interpretive limits

Falsification condition: withdraw a sensory-change claim if a changed response criterion sufficiently explains the result.

A starting task intermixes signal-present and signal-absent trials and records hits and false alarms together. Adding residue requires distinguishing the preceding outcome's influence on the next response from current stimulus intensity. Separating sensory limits from decision criteria is central here.

#### 6.1 A hypothetical detection task

Suppose participants detecting faint sounds are told that missing one has a high cost. More detection reports do not establish improved auditory reception. False alarms, confidence, and reaction time are compared together. This illustrates investigating how residue and boundary rules may change observations; performance is judged once the corresponding data and computations exist.
