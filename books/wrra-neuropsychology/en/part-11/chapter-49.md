# Chapter 49 How Might Consciousness Arise and Persist, and How Can It Be Assessed?


### 1 Scene and Question

The same person stops responding under anesthesia and later responds again. Their body and all long-term memories have not disappeared and been recreated. What changed temporarily, and what remained? This chapter separates onset, persistence, interruption, and recovery into organization and execution conditions, then locates measurements within those distinctions.

### 2 The Starting Point in Existing Research

Standardized behavioral measures such as the CRS-R organize observations under clinical conditions. Repeated assessments and confounder checks matter; a total score should not be read as the subjective richness of any possible entity. PCI calculates complexity of TMS-evoked EEG responses under a defined procedure. These approaches have different inputs, measurements, and interpretations, rather than being measurements of one quantity in a shared unit. [R74, R78]

Multidimensional accounts of animal consciousness also caution against compressing every difference into a single level. How strong the evidence for experience is and how rich particular characteristics might be are separate axes. This distinction is the book's reporting rule, informed by R81's multidimensional approach. [R81]

### 3 Verification Inputs

Inputs include trial-level stimuli, reports, behavior, neural responses, transition times, medication and sleep conditions, and structural damage and recovery records. Resources B retain their units, such as time, memory, or energy; subjective fatigue is not automatically entered as the same quantity. Unmeasured resources are marked hypothetical. No new patient dataset or neural simulation of consciousness onset is included.

### 4 WRRA-Specific Transformation

#### 4.1 Onset, maintenance, interruption, and recovery

Onset concerns a candidate executable organization and experience-related observations becoming established from declared initial conditions. Maintenance concerns continuing its functions under allowed perturbations and costs. Interruption distinguishes stopping execution, access, or report; recovery identifies what returns using retained G, R, and records. Bodily persistence alone does not establish continuous experience, and interrupted reporting does not establish structural destruction.

Inheritance is recorded as G_(k+1)=Inherit_D(G_k, amendment_k, retained_state_k). Declare the analysis unit and owner, carrier, inherited components, permissible changes, reconstruction procedure, and compatibility checks. This is a Core 2.1 interface, not an equation establishing a single brain mechanism. Whether a memory change is treated as a state change or structural change depends on the declared timescale and task. [W3]

#### 4.2 Resources and maintenance

The Core condition B_j−Cbound_j(a)−Hbound_j(a)≥Reserve_j includes bounds on action costs and checking, recording, and response costs, together with reserves. It specifies execution authorization, not a consciousness threshold. Connecting resource limitations to consciousness maintenance requires application rules and observations specifying which function deteriorates first. Costs of bodily maintenance and additional experience-related processing must not be counted twice. [W3]

#### 4.3 Report two assessment axes

The first axis concerns evidence for experience: test conditions, positive and negative findings, sensory and motor pathways, alternatives, and evidence types. Unvalidated weights must not generate numerical claims such as an 80% probability of consciousness. The second axis describes characteristics such as perceptual discrimination, value-sensitive choices, temporal retention, and self-related access. Measurement comparability across humans, animals, and artificial systems requires separate assessment.

#### 4.4 Using the past and generating goals

The Paradox of Deficiency and this section meet at the question of how a goal arises. A deficiency is a gap between the current state and a state to be maintained or reached. Detecting the gap identifies a problem and can prompt a search for relevant traces of past experience, denoted by R. Recall makes possible responses available. Together, deficiency and memory can support the formation of a specific goal.

Imagine noticing that you are hungry and often skip meals after work. You remember being too tired to cook after getting home, but also recall having eaten on days when you packed a lunch the previous evening. You decide to pack tomorrow’s lunch tonight. Hunger identified the problem, and memory helped determine what to do and when. Checking whether the action reduced the deficiency leaves a new trace for later decisions. Forming a goal in this way is the proposed threshold at score 5.

| Score | Observation criterion |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | Retains a physical trace left by a past event. |
| 1 | Responds differently to the same input because of a retained change. |
| 2 | Retrieves a specific record in response to a current cue. |
| 3 | Uses the contents of a retrieved record to change a present choice. |
| 4 | Selectively searches for relevant records in pursuit of a given goal. |
| 5 | Connects an internal deficiency with past experience to form a specific goal that was not externally assigned. |
| 6 | Attributes an experience to its own history and distinguishes it from information acquired from others. |
| 7 | Connects experiences across time to understand changes in itself and guide future action. |
| 8 | Examines the reliability of memory and its own judgment and revises subsequent search or decision rules. |

Assess the function observed within a specified system boundary and situation. Record the highest function supported by the observation and mark other functions as unverified where appropriate. Evidence for a higher function does not establish every lower one. Scores are ordinal labels and cannot be added or converted into a measure of experiential richness.

| Case | Score | Observation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scratched stone | 0 | A trace of impact remains. |
| Bent metal sheet | 1 | Prior deformation alters its response to the same force. |
| Fault log display | 2 | An alert retrieves the corresponding log. |
| Recovery controller | 3 | Log contents determine the recovery procedure. |
| AI searching records on request | 4 | A supplied task guides the search for relevant records. |
| Person planning meal preparation | 5 | Hunger and missed meals inform a newly chosen goal. |
| Person checking the source of a memory | 6 | Distinguishes a firsthand event from a report. |
| Person reviewing changing habits | 7 | Connects experiences over time to guide the next action. |

A robot vacuum illustrates the boundary. Returning to the dock at a fixed battery threshold follows a preset rule. Using route history to change the path still serves the same charging goal. A possible score-5 case would require an execution record showing that the system used its low-power state and prior failures to formulate a different objective, such as finishing a particular area in place of cleaning the entire floor. Vary battery state and operating history separately to test the contribution of each.

#### 4.5 Deficiency and memory in living systems

Amoebae lack neurons. Membrane receptors and intracellular signaling connect internal state with external signals in amoeboid cells. In earthworms, ganglia and the ventral nerve cord coordinate sensation and movement across segments. Vertebrate brain circuits can combine bodily state, past experience, and possible actions. Apply the same functional question to each observed behavior.

| Organism and behavior | Implementation and assessment |
| --- | --- |
| Dictyostelium under starvation | Amoeboid cells exchange cAMP signals and aggregate. Nutrient shortage changes behavior. The response alone does not demonstrate retrieval of a past record. |
| Plant wound response | In Arabidopsis, leaf damage triggers calcium signaling to other leaves and induces defense responses. Separate the effects of past injury from those of the present signal. |
| Earthworm under repeated touch | A change in response to identical touch is a score-1 candidate if it persists beyond stimulation and sensory adaptation or muscular fatigue can be excluded. |
| Amphibian spatial search | Finding a previously used site from a new starting point can test use of spatial information. Control current visual and odor cues before considering scores 2–3. |
| Reptile learning and retention | A turtle choosing a learned cue after a delay can be tested for use of past information. Move the cue or vary reward contingencies to distinguish cue use from a directional habit. |
| Ape preparation for future tool use | Selecting and retaining a tool for later use illustrates preparation for a future task. An experimenter-provided task leaves the origin of the goal to be assessed separately. |

In mammalian feeding, hypothalamic circuits help link energy state to behavior, hippocampal circuits contribute to recall of places and events, and networks that include prefrontal regions participate in selection and planning. These functions overlap across circuits. The WRRA categories of deficiency, residue, and update provide a way to compare their roles. Activity in one region alone cannot establish that a goal was formed.

To separate the inputs, compare four conditions that cross current food availability or feeding state with different histories of where food was obtained. Keep present cues constant. Returning to a former food location can support a memory-use interpretation. A score-5 candidate also requires evidence that deficiency and memory contributed to a specific goal that was not assigned externally. Record evidence of subjective experience on the separate axis described in section 4.3.

Sources: Toyota et al., Science (2018), doi:10.1126/science.aat7744. Mulcahy and Call, Science (2006), doi:10.1126/science.1125456. The earthworm, amphibian, and reptile rows are conditional assessment examples.

#### 4.6 Ways nervous systems connect information

The following cases compare different lineages by the presence of neurons, where signals converge, and how past experience and present state influence behavior.

| Organism and organization | Observation and assessment |
| --- | --- |
| Sponges without neurons | Cells associated with secretory signaling occur near digestive choanocytes. The finding concerns coordination among cells. Use of past records remains unverified. |
| Placozoans without neurons or synapses | Several cell types secrete peptides. These animals offer a comparison for whole-body coordination without neurons. Coordination alone does not establish memory use. |
| Box jellyfish with nerve nets and sensory structures | Associating visual cues with collisions changes obstacle avoidance. Experiments also examined the rhopalia. This is a score-1 candidate because experience changes the response. |
| C. elegans with a compact nervous system | Search changes with time since leaving food. Recent food history and neural transmission can be studied together. Goal generation requires a separate test. |
| Larval zebrafish with a vertebrate brain | Feeding state affects visual processing and action selection. This provides a way to examine current need under a shared stimulus. The contribution of prior history is a separate question. |
| Octopus with a central brain and distributed arm circuits | Experiments report avoidance of a place associated with injury and preference for a place associated with pain relief. Learned changes are score-1 candidates. Evidence about pain experience belongs on the separate experience axis. |
| Scrub-jay with an avian brain | Experiments examine caching where food will be unavailable later. To consider score 5, distinguish the contribution of learned contingencies and experimenter-arranged conditions from the formation of a goal. |

Food search in the nematode illustrates the effect of recent experience, while visual choice in zebrafish illustrates the effect of present feeding state. Varying both in the same design would help separate their respective contributions to goal formation.

#### 4.7 An experimental design for goal formation

Verification inputs. Present the same scenario to all participants: an uncommitted hour after work. Randomly assign participants to one of four conditions. Cross an imagined tired evening with an imagined usual evening, and recall of an actual similar evening with recall of an unrelated everyday event. Hold the setting and available time, including recall time, constant. Imagined fatigue is the manipulation in this example. Physiological fatigue would require a separate design.

WRRA transformation. Before the recall task, ask participants to describe what they currently lack and what outcome they would like by the end of the hour. After recall, ask them to state the desired outcome again and identify which part of the recalled experience influenced it. Then ask for a first action. Participants may report no deficiency or no goal. The researcher assigns no specific goal.

Outputs and scoring. Two raters unaware of condition assignment independently classify the goal, the first action, and their relation. A newly formed goal or a change in desired outcome counts as goal updating. A different action serving the same outcome counts as a change of means. Record a score-5 candidate when the response links a deficiency and a specific past experience to the formation of an externally unassigned goal, and the first action fits that goal. Mark insufficient evidence as indeterminate. Report inter-rater agreement and reasons for disagreement.

Falsification conditions. Compare rates of score-5 candidates and means changes across all four conditions. A change restricted to means does not support goal generation. Examine whether relevant recall and the deficiency condition together yield more goal formation. If the design yields no discernible condition difference, record that it did not obtain supporting evidence. This section is a protocol example for a study yet to be run.

### 5 Outputs and Execution Record

The completed outputs are transition specifications and a two-axis reporting procedure. Subsequent studies could measure retention across delays, access-recovery times after perturbation, and performance changes after removing connections. These measure declared functions, not experience in isolation. Clinical validity and cross-species measurement validity of new indicators were not established in this edition.

### 6 Falsification Conditions and Interpretive Boundaries

If a function persists without an organization claimed necessary for maintenance, revise the necessity claim. A recovery model that classifies states but fails on intervention outcomes is incomplete as a control model. If reproduction requires removing resource constraints, implementation under finite resources has not been established. Functional persistence and continuity of the same subject remain separate issues.

### 7 Current Verdict

The Core 2.1 onset, maintenance, inheritance, and reporting specifications are INFERRED. Sufficient conditions for experience, a universal consciousness scale, and clinical control effects remain OPEN. The resource gate is not used as a consciousness detector.
