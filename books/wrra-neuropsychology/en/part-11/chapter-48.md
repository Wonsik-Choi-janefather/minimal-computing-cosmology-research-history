# Chapter 48 Could Objects and Artificial Systems Be Conscious?


### 1 Scene and Question

A stone changes when heated, a thermostat responds to temperature, and a learning agent changes its rules through experience. Each undergoes state changes, but does that establish the same kind of experience? This chapter asks whether human cells are necessary for consciousness and what organization must be specified to investigate the question across materials.

### 2 The Starting Point in Existing Research

A functionalist perspective makes causal organization a relevant comparison target. Describing functional roles and establishing phenomenal experience nevertheless remain distinct issues. Panpsychist positions do not simply assert that every object thinks like a person; they also face questions about the relationship between fundamental experience and composite subjects. This book includes those positions as topics of inquiry without treating philosophical possibilities as established measurements. [R73]

Butlin and colleagues (2023) extracted indicators relevant to artificial systems from several consciousness theories. This is a theory-based assessment framework, not a universally validated machine-consciousness test. Claiming consciousness in conversation, producing complex outputs, possessing an organization, and having experience must be distinguished. This external work supplies comparisons; its indicators are not assumed already implemented by the Core. [R84]

### 3 Verification Inputs

For artificial systems, distinguish program specifications from actual executions. Relevant inputs include sensory pathways, memory writing and reading, recurrence, timescales, access to the system's own state, action–environment feedback, computational resources, and logs. Biological systems require corresponding measurements and interventions. When stones or thermostats serve as comparisons, the analyst must not insert nonexistent memories or goals merely by describing them in intentional language.

### 4 WRRA-Specific Transformation

#### 4.1 Why extend the application to Core 2.1?

Core 1.0 input, state, residue, updating, and phenotype provide a starting point for specifying an execution. The questions now broaden: what counts as the same organization, what is inherited into a subsequent execution, and how do an output-only intervention and a rule-changing intervention differ? Core 2.1's G/P distinction, structural inheritance, and intervention contracts make those questions explicit. This motivates the extension. A higher version number does not establish substrate independence or automatically revalidate the earlier calculations. [W1, W3]

The general interface is P_k=Render_D(G_k, X_k, R_k, Boundary_k, B_k, input_k), where D is a declared domain specification. G is not restricted to DNA; it concerns presently instantiated organization that can continue into the next execution. This is neither a universal physical equation nor a completed consciousness equation. The application asks which D preserves the relevant distinctions in experience-related observations. [W3]

#### 4.2 What must survive a change of material?

Memory, recurrent updating, sharing across information channels, and use of self-related state can be considered candidate features. Arbitrarily assembling them does not establish a sufficient condition for consciousness. First specify what each explains and what should change when it is removed. If carrier C changes, test whether directionality, temporal characteristics, channel identity, ownership, and required correlations are preserved. Identical surface outputs do not establish identical internal organization.

A structural intervention changes the G used by subsequent executions. An output intervention changes present P without necessarily changing the inherited rules. Producing consciousness-related behavior once and maintaining its generating organization are different tests. Q(a|S_k) is an authorization gate for action-resource and contract requirements, not a consciousness indicator: Q=1 does not mean conscious, and Q=0 does not mean unconscious. [W3]

### 5 Outputs and Execution Record

The output is a structural comparison specification and a proposed removal–restoration study. Comparing maintenance, restoration, and changed-condition responses in systems with similar outputs can provide a richer functional explanation than superficial resemblance. No such artificial system was implemented here, and no conscious machine was produced. Earlier agents in the author's consciousness manuscript are referenced as functional toy models. Historical numerical reports whose logs and code were not recovered and rerun are not presented as newly verified results. [W4]

### 6 Falsification Conditions and Interpretive Boundaries

A necessary-feature claim requires revision if the specified function survives removal of that feature. If a structure claimed sufficient fails to produce the specified observation under appropriate functional conditions, that application fails. Where the hypothesis offers no observation distinguishing presence from absence of experience, functional success alone cannot complete the ontological assessment. The possibility of object consciousness remains open without presupposing its existence.
