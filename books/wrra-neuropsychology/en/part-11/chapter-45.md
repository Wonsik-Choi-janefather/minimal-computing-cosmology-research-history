# Chapter 45 What Is Consciousness? Definitions in Philosophy, Psychology, and Medicine


### 1 Scene and Question

A person with closed eyes does not answer. They might be asleep, awake but unable to move, or dreaming. In this hypothetical scene, silence alone cannot establish whether experience is present. This chapter asks what we intend to explain by “consciousness” and which observations bear on that explanation. Without specifying the target, responsiveness, arousal, self-report, and subjective experience can circularly substitute for one another.

### 2 The Starting Point in Existing Research

#### 2.1 Experience, access, and self-consciousness

Nagel (1974) framed the problem of there being a subjective character to being a particular creature. Block (1995) distinguished the character of experience from information being available for reasoning, report, and action: phenomenal consciousness and access consciousness, respectively. How they connect remains a research question; defining the former solely through articulate speech would remove that question by stipulation. Chalmers (1995) distinguished explaining functions from explaining why experience accompanies them. These are philosophical formulations, not experimental confirmation of a particular neural mechanism. [R71–R73]

Self-consciousness includes different ways of treating one's body, actions, and thoughts as related to oneself. Attention allocates processing to information. These functions may contribute to reporting conscious contents, but consciousness, attention, and self-consciousness do not receive one interchangeable definition here. We separately ask what experience is present, how information can be used, and how the system represents itself.

#### 2.2 Philosophical positions and clinical distinctions

Substance dualism emphasizes an ontological distinction between mind and matter; physicalism concerns mental phenomena's relationship to the physical world; functionalism emphasizes functional and causal organization. Phenomenological approaches examine how experience is given and structured, while panpsychist positions explore a fundamental status for experience. These descriptions orient comparisons among families of positions; they do not erase differences within each family. No position is assumed to follow immediately from the Core. Calling something a phenotype does not by itself resolve the ontological relationship between experience and physical execution. [R73]

Clinical assessment distinguishes arousal from evidence of awareness of oneself or the environment and considers the conditions under which behavior is assessed. Opening the eyes, following commands, and communicating are different observations. Coma involves an absence of observed wakefulness and awareness; unresponsive wakefulness syndrome involves wakefulness without established behavioral evidence of awareness; a minimally conscious state involves limited but discernible evidence of awareness. These distinctions depend on examination conditions and repeated observation. Disorders-of-consciousness guidelines emphasize repeated standardized assessment and consideration of confounders. Clinical classifications assess patients; they are not philosophical definitions applying to every possible entity. [R74]

### 4 WRRA-Specific Transformation

Core 2.1 writes the state as S_k=(G_k, X_k, R_k, B_k, L_k). G is the organization of executable relations, rules, and capabilities; X is current operating state; R comprises presently retained effects; B denotes available resources; L is the provenance and execution ledger. G may be represented within X, so the same structure must not be counted twice. These definitions come from Wonsik Choi and Jeongin Choi's Core text. The consciousness application below is this book's domain specification. [W3]

The author's earlier proposition that consciousness is a phenotype is read as a hypothesis: rather than identifying consciousness with every internal state, investigate it as an experiential aspect instantiated under particular structures and conditions. The Core's general phenotype P is not automatically identical to conscious experience. An experiential phenotype E can be proposed separately from behavioral and reporting phenotypes P, but E is the target of an experience-related hypothesis, not a directly measured value. Introducing E here as an auxiliary domain symbol does not amend the Core state. Rules connecting E with observables O must be specified and tested for each task. [W3, W4]

The transformation proceeds from verification inputs to distinct hypotheses about experience, access, and report, then to G/X/R/B and observation-pathway specifications, and finally to comparisons of observable outcomes. Establishing that an organization generates reports and claiming that it has experience belong to different explanatory contracts. Language reports are important evidence, but are neither a sole definition nor a necessary condition for every species.

### 5 Outputs and Execution Record

The completed output is a conceptual specification separating experience, functional access, self-related processing, arousal, and report. Future data could support an evidence table linking observations to individual claims. This edition produces neither a new consciousness index nor a neural-data analysis.

### 6 Falsification Conditions and Interpretive Boundaries

A rule requiring a particular report for consciousness needs reconsideration if other pathways preserve evidence of experience when that reporting pathway is blocked. Conversely, a rule admitting every behavior as evidence has no discriminatory power. Failure of a functional specification is distinguished from refutation of an ontological position. Claims that produce no testable difference remain philosophical interpretations without an empirical verdict.
