# Chapter 46 What Have Experiments Established About Consciousness?


### 1 Scene and Question

A person who says they did not see a stimulus identifies its location above chance. Conversely, another person may understand a command without being able to respond bodily. This chapter asks what can be concluded when performance, report, and neural responses diverge. Individual findings are read for the functions they separate rather than expanded into a single proof about consciousness as a whole.

### 2 The Starting Point in Existing Research

#### 2.1 Separating seeing from processing

Weiskrantz and colleagues (1974) investigated visual performance in a patient with an occipital lesion, within a visual field conventionally assessed as blind. The case motivates distinguishing discrimination performance from reports of seeing. It does not imply that every instance of blindsight has the same character or that one examination excludes all possibilities of weak experience. Thresholds, confidence, and forced-choice conditions matter. [R75]

Masking and subliminal-processing research addresses whether unreported stimuli can still affect processing. Dehaene and colleagues' (2006) taxonomy is a theoretical organization distinguishing stimulus strength and conditions of access. It provides a comparison framework against treating all unreported processing as identical; the entire taxonomy is not presented as the result of one experiment. [R76]

#### 2.2 Integration, sleep, and limitations of responsiveness

Sperry (1968) connected dissociations in information access and responses after cerebral disconnection with the unity of consciousness. Task-specific dissociation cannot simply be converted into an observed count of exactly two subjects within one body. What was presented to each hemisphere and how responses were obtained must first be specified. [R77]

TMS–EEG research comparing conditions including sleep and anesthesia used the complexity of evoked responses to assess consciousness-related states. Lack of response to external stimulation differs from absence of internal experience; records such as dream recollection also require separating the experienced state from the time of report. PCI is defined by a particular stimulation, measurement, and analysis procedure in human brains, not a law stating that every complex signal is conscious. [R78]

#### 2.3 Covert command following and theory comparison

In Bodien and colleagues' (2024) multicenter study, task-based EEG or fMRI detected command following in 60 of 241 participants without observed behavioral command following. Within that sample and those test conditions, motor responses did not capture all cognitive capacity. Only 43 of 112 behavioral command followers showed responses on the corresponding neural tests; a negative test therefore also cannot establish absence of consciousness. [R79]

Global neuronal workspace theory emphasizes information becoming broadly accessible for flexible use across processing systems. Integrated information theory centers on a system's intrinsic causal organization and its integration and differentiation. Saying that both integrate information does not make them identical, and renaming their variables as WRRA carriers or residue does not constitute integration. [R80]

The Cogitate Consortium (2025) collaboratively tested prespecified predictions of these theories. Results must be read prediction by prediction and within the measurement conditions, not converted into a declaration that either theory finally solves consciousness. The study did not test WRRA and cannot establish WRRA's empirical adequacy. [R80]

### 3 Verification Inputs

Required inputs include participant characteristics, stimulus delivery, task comprehension, sensory and motor pathways, medication and arousal variation, trial-level behavior, neural signals, and reporting times. Trials with no performance and trials with measurement failure must be separated. Sensitivity and specificity are reported with their target population. This chapter reviews published studies without reanalyzing their raw data.

### 4 WRRA-Specific Transformation

The same observable O can arise in at least three situations: experience-related processing is weak; processing occurs without access to report; or a report is intended but its output pathway is blocked. A Core 2.1 application specifies these alternatives through distinctions among G connectivity, X activation, B limitations, and R retained effects. One observation cannot uniquely invert those possibilities. [W3]

A proposed test cross-checks different reporting pathways. Disagreements between behavioral reports and neural command following are retained rather than deleted as errors, allowing comparison of structural and observation-pathway assumptions. Successful classification is a state-estimation achievement. Explaining and testing outcomes after changing stimulation or medication is an additional intervention-prediction achievement. This organization is the book's WRRA application, not an implementation of an external consciousness theory's mathematics.

### 5 Outputs and Execution Record

The output is a design distinguishing input, access, maintenance, reporting, and intervention for each experiment. Planned outputs include condition-specific performance, report distributions, neural responses, and pathway-disagreement rates. Beyond the earlier hypothetical computations in Chapters 41–42 and Appendix D, no neural model for this chapter was executed and no patient data were reanalyzed.

### 6 Falsification Conditions and Interpretive Boundaries

If an application specifies that blocking a connection removes access but repeatedly encounters preserved access, that connection hypothesis requires revision. A model explaining every disagreement through hidden states without specifying additional observations remains difficult to identify. Explaining existing data and generating outcomes for new conditions under fixed rules are recorded separately.
