# Chapter 6 Brain Structure and Functional Networks

### 1 Scene and question

Naming a brain region differs from explaining a psychological function. Increased activity in a region does not establish that a person necessarily experienced one particular emotion, because the region may participate in several tasks. This problem of reverse inference is important in interpreting neuroimaging. (Poldrack, 2006 [R6](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2005.12.004))

Chapter question: What level of function can be inferred from brain locations and networks?

### 2 Starting points in established research

#### 2.1 Why learn structure?

The cerebral cortex, subcortical structures, cerebellum, and brainstem are interconnected parts of the nervous system. Lobes help locate structures but do not divide complex mental functions into an equivalent number of boxes. Several connections may participate in a task. Readers should use a brain map while also tracing inputs, outputs, and interacting pathways. (Spielman et al., 2020, §3.4 [R1](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/3-4-the-brain-and-spinal-cord))

#### 2.2 Which instrument reads what?

EEG records electrical signals at the scalp. The BOLD signal in functional MRI is an indirect indicator related to changes in blood oxygenation. Temporal and spatial resolution, noise, and preprocessing differ. (Logothetis et al., 2001 [R35](https://doi.org/10.1038/35084005))

#### 2.3 Learning from lesions and interventions

Changes in performance after brain damage provide important evidence about functional roles. However, lesion extent, changes in other connections, and recovery may be mixed together. Preserved performance in one task alongside difficulty in another motivates examining their separation, while alternatives such as differences in task difficulty must also be checked. (Scoville and Milner, 1957 [R10](https://doi.org/10.1136/jnnp.20.1.11))

#### 2.4 Original research comparing BOLD with neural activity

Logothetis and colleagues compared BOLD responses, local field potentials, and single- and multi-unit firing in monkey visual cortex. The comparison shows that BOLD reads neural activity through a hemodynamic pathway and is not identical to firing counts. Interpreting an indicator requires specifying the task and measurement path. (Logothetis et al., 2001 [R35](https://doi.org/10.1038/35084005))

Scope of evidence: a function connecting WRRA states to imaging observables specifies measurement paths and delays. Findings in monkey visual cortex do not by themselves complete connections to every human emotion task.

### 4 WRRA-specific transformation

Transformation specification: distinguish and connect observation paths at each measurement level.

WRRA records relationships alongside locations. It asks which inputs arrived, which connections transmitted change, and what conditions existed in other circuits and the body. Statistical relationships obtained through functional connectivity are not automatically restated as direct anatomical connections or causal influences.

#### 4.1 Criteria for connecting circuits and psychology

Accurately classifying a subsequent choice from circuit activity does not establish that the circuit caused the choice. Classification on external evaluation data, actual differences between options, and causal interventions are reported separately. Output differences between a model with a circuit connection and one without it show dependence within that model. The same dependence in a natural circuit requires separate validation.

#### 4.2 Which instrument reads what? — WRRA interpretation

The two measurements do not read the same state in the same way. WRRA specifies measurement procedures and temporal alignment, and does not equate indicator changes with neural firing itself.

#### 4.3 Learning from lesions and interventions — WRRA interpretation

WRRA treats lesion location, state transition, and observation as separate terms.

### 6 Falsification conditions and interpretive limits

Falsification condition: restrict a single-function interpretation if the same imaging pattern occurs in other tasks.

Connecting brain science and psychology from this perspective requires observation paths across levels. Imaging signals, task performance, and personal reports are connected through their respective measurement models. Even when all three covary, the conditions under which agreement holds must be examined.
