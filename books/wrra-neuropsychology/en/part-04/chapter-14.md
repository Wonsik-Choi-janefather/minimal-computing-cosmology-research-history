# Chapter 14 Observational Learning, Skill Acquisition, and Habits

### 1 Scene and question

People learn by observing others as well as through their own trial and error. Bandura and colleagues' classic experiment investigated relationships between a model's observed behavior and children's subsequent behavior. (Bandura et al., 1961 [R9](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0045925)) Findings from individual studies should not be generalized to imitation or personality change in every situation.

Chapter question: How can what was observed, what was learned, and what was actually executed be distinguished?

### 2 Starting points in established research

#### 2.1 Observation and actual performance

Someone may carefully observe and remember behavior but lack the capacity or opportunity to perform it now. Separating acquisition from expression allows investigation of this difference. Understanding instructions, executing movements, and displaying learning under changed rewards are separate questions. (Spielman et al., 2020, §6.4 [R1](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/6-4-observational-learning-modeling))

#### 2.2 Practice arrangement and feedback

Performance during practice differs from delayed retention. Roediger and Karpicke compared delayed memory after rereading material and after taking recall tests. That paper does not also validate every claim about practice spacing and task diversity. (Roediger and Karpicke, 2006 [R32](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x))

#### 2.3 Questions for identifying habits

Repeated behavior in a familiar situation may reflect reevaluation of a goal or easy elicitation by a cue. Changing outcome value and action–outcome relationships offers one possible test. One test does not classify all everyday habits. (Wood and Rünger, 2016 [R46](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-122414-033417))

#### 2.4 What the classic observational-learning experiment showed

Bandura and colleagues assigned 72 young children to an aggressive model, a nonaggressive model, or no model. Behavior was then recorded in another play situation without the model; more model-like behavior appeared after observation of the aggressive model. The finding concerns imitation under those specified conditions. (Bandura et al., 1961 [R9](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0045925))

Scope of evidence: this supports studying observational input separately from later expression. Imitation in one experiment does not directly establish lifelong personality or all everyday aggression.

### 4 WRRA-specific transformation

Transformation specification: separate acquired residue from current execution conditions.

Observed behavior is input; retained representations or action tendencies are residue; actual performance is phenotype passing through current capacities and permissible conditions. Observation, inferred acquisition, and observed execution remain distinct. Skill acquisition also requires specifying practice arrangement and feedback.

#### 4.1 Benefits and costs of automatization

Skills requiring less attention may leave capacity for other tasks. However, continued familiar behavior after rules change may increase error costs. Minimal computation considers both rapid familiar execution and revision under new conditions. Checks and reserve resources needed for stability and flexibility also count as costs.

#### 4.2 Observation and actual performance — WRRA interpretation

Unobserved behavior does not directly establish absent learning.

#### 4.3 Practice arrangement and feedback — WRRA interpretation

WRRA distinguishes successful performance with present assistance from structures retained for independent use. Removing assistance and conducting delayed tests can investigate the difference.

#### 4.4 Questions for identifying habits — WRRA interpretation

WRRA separates cues, goals, and consequences to specify the required update.

### 5 Outputs and execution record

The current output is an explanatory specification of phenomena, variables, and validation paths. Planned outputs: imitation, skilled performance, and habitual choice.

### 6 Falsification conditions and interpretive limits

Falsification condition: reconsider a strong habit interpretation if behavior responds flexibly to changed outcome values.

Repetition alone does not sufficiently define habit. Researchers can examine maintenance under changes in contextual cues and outcome value. Low-attention execution may connect to minimal computation, but automatic does not by itself mean optimal or adaptive.
