# Chapter 3 Research Methods and Causal Inference

### 1 Scene and question

Suppose people who speak less in meetings also have higher anxiety scores. Anxiety may have caused silence, but an organization withholding speaking opportunities might have produced both. Covariation and the claim that changing one condition changes another require different evidence.

Chapter question: How can the influence of past experience be distinguished from present conditions?

### 2 Starting points in established research

Experiments manipulate and compare conditions; observations and surveys examine everyday variation that is difficult to manipulate. Longitudinal studies follow the same targets over time. Random assignment reduces systematic differences between comparison conditions but does not automatically eliminate attrition or measurement error. Sample scope and research setting must also enter interpretation. (Spielman et al., 2020, §2.2 [R1](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/2-2-approaches-to-research))

#### 2.1 From a question to a research design

A research question must become a measurable contrast. Asking whether failure reduces challenge-taking requires defining failure, the choice constituting a challenge, and the comparison time. Actual failure differs from believing one has failed; encountering a difficult task can also be distinguished from receiving negative feedback.

First specify the outcome and competing explanations. Earlier failure residue might influence it, or participants might rationally assess the next task's success probability as lower. Separating current difficulty from feedback information helps distinguish these paths. A useful research question shows which comparison separates explanations.

#### 2.2 Observation, interviews, surveys, and case studies

Everyday observation reveals contexts difficult to capture in a laboratory. Interviews explore participants' meanings; surveys examine response distributions to common questions. Case studies document rare or complex pathways in detail. Each method accesses some evidence and may miss other evidence. (Spielman et al., 2020, §2.2 [R1](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/2-2-approaches-to-research))

No method guarantees causality by its name. Observational studies may strengthen causal inference using temporal ordering and natural changes in conditions, while experiments may become difficult to interpret if manipulation differs from participants' actual experience. (Spielman et al., 2020, §2.3 [R1](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/2-3-analyzing-findings))

#### 2.3 What random assignment does

Random assignment sets participants' probabilities of entering comparison conditions according to the design. It helps reduce confounding in sufficiently large samples, although chance imbalances can remain in small samples. Recruitment and assignment are separate: good assignment does not remove generalization limits arising from recruitment from one particular population. (Spielman et al., 2020, §2.2 [R1](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/2-2-approaches-to-research))

Task instructions, experimenter behavior, equipment, and measurement times should be matched, with assessor blinding considered where possible. Checks are also needed on how participants understood the manipulation. A manipulation check may itself influence subsequent behavior, so its timing belongs in the design. (Spielman et al., 2020, §2.2 [R1](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/2-2-approaches-to-research))

#### 2.4 Between-person and within-person comparisons

Between-group designs assign different people to different conditions. Within-person designs expose the same person to several conditions. The latter reduce individual differences but may mix practice, fatigue, and carryover into later conditions. This motivates varying order and specifying intervals between trials. (Spielman et al., 2020, §2.2 [R1](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/2-2-approaches-to-research))

#### 2.5 Longitudinal data and mediation paths

Measuring a candidate cause before an outcome is important but insufficient. A common cause may affect both processes. Repeated measurements also require intervals appropriate to the processes' speeds: daily records cannot directly establish the direction of second-scale connections. (Spielman et al., 2020, §2.3 [R1](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/2-3-analyzing-findings))

Suppose an intervention changes bodily responses and behavior. Explaining the bodily response as a mediator requires additional assumptions.

### 3 Validation inputs

Literature-supported design principles are distinguished from planned inputs. Planned inputs: experiential histories, manipulated conditions, and comparison-group data. The score example uses hypothetical values—Group A, 70→75; Group B, 50→60—not actual survey data.

### 4 WRRA-specific transformation

Transformation specification: estimate residue's contribution while controlling present conditions.

A WRRA claim about residue requires identifying past influence distinct from other present conditions. Comparing people with and without earlier failure does not identify its residue if current task difficulty and success probability also differ. Before experimentation, specify which term each manipulation changes and how other changes will be checked.

#### 4.1 Observation, interviews, surveys, and case studies — WRRA interpretation

The WRRA ledger records how data were collected and which conclusions that method permits.

In WRRA, order effects may be both noise to control and the residue under investigation. Decide beforehand which influence to control and which to measure. If an earlier condition continues to affect performance after the task changes, it can enter the model as part of present state. Residue to be removed and residue to be investigated are not treated as identical.

#### 4.2 Longitudinal data and mediation paths — WRRA interpretation

WRRA specifies intervention locations, intermediate states, output times, and observation paths, and compares alternatives. Drawing a path and identifying causality are separate achievements.

### 5 Outputs and execution record

Execution record: the hypothetical final-level difference is 75−60=15 points. Changes are 5 points for A and 10 for B. This arithmetic example shows how the question depends on the comparison quantity. Planned output: estimated condition differences and causal effects. No actual causal effect was estimated.

#### 5.1 A hypothetical example of score differences and changes

Suppose A's mean changes from 70 to 75 and B's from 50 to 60. A finishes 15 points higher, but its change is 5 points versus B's 10, so the difference in change points in the opposite direction. The calculations are simple; the comparison questions differ.

This does not prove that B's intervention caused the difference. Initial levels, measurement error, regression to the mean, and other experiences must be examined. Final level, improvement, and intervention effect call for different analyses. The comparison quantity appropriate to the purpose should be chosen before examining data.

### 6 Falsification conditions and interpretive limits

Falsification condition: narrow the causal claim if confounding factors or reverse-direction explanations account for the result.

#### 6.1 Distinguishing calibration, exploration, and confirmation

Revising a model while examining data is important exploration. Coefficients and structure may be calibrated against verified findings. Results used for calibration must, however, be distinguished from those evaluated with subsequently fixed rules. If the same data serve both roles, that scope is stated.

Preregistration records when questions and analysis plans were determined. Revisions and further exploration remain possible if reasons are documented. (Center for Open Science, n.d. [R22](https://www.cos.io/initiatives/prereg)) Registration itself guarantees neither a theory's truth nor an experiment's adequacy. WRRA research can evaluate explanatory consistency, implementation consistency, and failure conditions independently of whether it produces new predictions.

### 7 Current verdict

The arithmetic of the hypothetical values is COMPUTATIONAL PASS. It reproduces statistics and comparison quantities, not empirical validation of a WRRA-specific psychological mechanism. Correspondences in the research specification are INFERRED; evaluation using actual data is OPEN.

Chapter in one sentence: Covariation and outcomes caused by changing a condition require different causal evidence.

### 8 Next questions and evidence

Next research question: With present difficulty controlled, does changing only the order of earlier experience change the result?

#### 8.1 Specifying a first behavioral experiment

A possible design independently manipulates earlier feedback and current difficulty in a neutral, nonclinical task. Outcomes are challenge choice and reaction time; learning history and instruction comprehension are recorded. Sample size, tolerances, and exclusions are determined by purpose and expected variation. This book does not report executing that design.

The study could examine whether earlier feedback makes a difference when present information is matched, and how well a residue rule reproduces it. If present difficulty is sufficient, the residue claim is reduced. The point is to specify which result changes which connection, rather than constructing an explanation compatible with every possible outcome.
