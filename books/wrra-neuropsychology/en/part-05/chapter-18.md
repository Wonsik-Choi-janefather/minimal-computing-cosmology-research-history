# Chapter 18 Judgment, Decision Making, Uncertainty, and Cognitive Bias

### 1 Scene and question

People must choose even without sufficient evidence. Readily recalled examples or familiar forms may serve as judgment cues. Tversky and Kahneman systematically studied heuristics and biases in judgment. (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974 [R11](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124)) Bias observed in one task should not be expanded into irrationality in every situation.

Chapter question: Does the same information lead to different choices under different reference points and constraints?

### 2 Starting points in established research

#### 2.1 Probability, value, and choice

In constructing this book's choice models, outcome probability and magnitude, waiting time, and current resources are separate candidate variables. This is the author's design for specifying comparisons, not the conclusion of one experiment. Expected mean outcomes, tolerable risk, and personal goals are distinguished; choice-value definitions and rules transforming them into behavior are recorded separately.

#### 2.2 Reference points in judgment

The book examines hypotheses in which previous expectations or present circumstances serve as comparison standards. Reference points must be determined independently of outcomes, or competing reference-point models compared. Changing them afterward to fit every choice makes explanatory performance hard to assess. Treating WRRA residue as a candidate reference point is the author's proposal, not an established identity between the concepts.

#### 2.3 Bias and environmental fit

Judging from readily recalled examples may be fast and useful in one environment and produce errors in another. Evaluation requires the actual information distribution and the goal demanded. (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974 [R11](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124))

#### 2.4 Evidence connecting choice observations to theory

Kahneman and Tversky examined preference patterns under risk that departed from expected utility theory's descriptive account and proposed prospect theory. Its structure distinguishes a value function evaluating changes relative to a reference point from probability-related decision weights. This constitutes theory construction for explaining human choice. (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979 [R57](https://doi.org/10.2307/1914185))

Scope of evidence: WRRA residue's involvement in reference-point formation is a separate hypothesis. Connections between the accounts are assessed only to the extent that reference points and weights were set beforehand and used to compute and compare choice data.

### 4 WRRA-specific transformation

Transformation specification: fix rules through which current information and residue influence choice value.

WRRA records information actually accessible at choice time, residue, and time and action boundaries. Computation is reconstructed under those conditions rather than condemning choices with hindsight. This does not assume every choice was optimal: goals and costs are specified beforehand and used for assessment.

#### 4.1 Bias and environmental fit — WRRA interpretation

WRRA compares computation saved and errors produced within one contract. Researchers must not assume participants knew everything the researchers know.

### 5 Outputs and execution record

The current output is an explanatory specification of phenomena, variables, and validation paths. Planned outputs: choice probabilities, losses, and reaction times.

### 6 Falsification conditions and interpretive limits

Falsification condition: reject a rule if observations repeatedly run in the opposite direction after conditions are fixed.

Explaining previous losses requires separating current wealth, risk exposure, and information changes. The same loss history may lead one person to avoidance and another to further challenges. Residue's sign and magnitude are research questions; opposite outcomes are not both declared successful afterward.

#### 6.1 Testing changed conditions

The same person can receive different information displays or choice deadlines. Measuring probability comprehension, actual choices, and confidence together can distinguish representation from preference. Rules fitted in one condition are applied to another display format. If they fail, the altered information transformation is revised and its scope stated.
