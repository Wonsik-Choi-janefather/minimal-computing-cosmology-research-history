# Chapter 20 Intelligence, Cognitive Abilities, Individual Differences, and Measurement

### 1 Scene and question

Cognitive tests estimate aspects of ability through tasks performed under particular conditions. Language comprehension, reasoning, memory, and processing speed can be distinguished. Score meanings depend on standardization populations and procedures, measurement error, and intended use. (Spielman et al., 2020, §7.5 [R1](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/7-5-measures-of-intelligence))

Chapter question: Under what conditions can individual cognitive differences be compared fairly?

### 2 Starting points in established research

#### 2.1 Common and specific abilities

Correlations among cognitive tests can be analyzed for common factors and domain-specific characteristics. A statistically extracted factor summarizes observed relationships; it does not itself discover a neural organ or substance. (Spielman et al., 2020, §7.4 [R1](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/7-4-what-are-intelligence-and-creativity))

#### 2.2 Standardization and norms

Score interpretation requires knowing the population and procedures used to construct comparison standards. Raw scores, normed scores, and measurement-error ranges are considered together. Repeated testing can introduce task familiarity, so timing and previous test experience are recorded. Changed scores are not immediately read as increases in one ability. (Spielman et al., 2020, §7.5 [R1](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/7-5-measures-of-intelligence))

#### 2.3 Fair comparisons

Giving the same test to people differing in language, sensory conditions, or educational opportunity does not always provide equivalent measurement. Items may carry different meanings or burdens across groups. Validation and accessibility checks appropriate to the comparison are needed. (Putnick and Bornstein, 2016 [R44](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dr.2016.06.004))

#### 2.4 Measurement conditions for comparing equal scores

Putnick and Bornstein review procedures and reporting practices for measurement invariance across groups and times. Presenting identical items and establishing score comparability are separate matters. Permissible comparisons depend on the level of invariance examined. (Putnick and Bornstein, 2016 [R44](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dr.2016.06.004))

Scope of evidence: differences in measurement paths are examined alongside group means rather than translating mean differences directly into internal-state differences. Invariance does not mean every individual must respond identically.

### 4 WRRA-specific transformation

Transformation specification: separate persistent characteristics, present state, and task experience.

WRRA seeks to distinguish relatively persistent individual differences, test-day states, and task familiarity. Lower scores on a fatigued day do not establish a change in overall ability. Conversely, present state is not used to dismiss every persistent difference. Repeated measurements and relationships between tasks are needed.

#### 4.1 Common and specific abilities — WRRA interpretation

WRRA distinguishes test scores, latent characteristics, and neural implementation. A relationship between a common factor and common carrier is a separate connecting hypothesis.

#### 4.2 Fair comparisons — WRRA interpretation

Personal states, external support, and task boundaries specify the conditions whose outcomes were compared.

### 6 Falsification conditions and interpretive limits

Falsification condition: restrict direct comparison if measurement meanings differ across languages or populations.

Culture, language, and educational opportunity enter interpretation. Representing all a person's diverse capacities with one score requires correspondingly broad evidence. Nor does completing a test quickly establish high intelligence merely because it resembles minimal computation.

#### 6.1 Individual explanations and limits of use

Using tests for selection or educational support requires distinguishing research-level correlations from individual-level error. Predictive ability does not mean accurate classification of every person. WRRA models also assess purposes and misclassification costs separately. Explanatory simplicity or computational speed alone cannot justify consequential judgments about individuals.
