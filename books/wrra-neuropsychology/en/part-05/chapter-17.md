# Chapter 17 Concepts, Reasoning, Problem Solving, and Creativity

### 1 Scene and question

Recognizing an unfamiliar object as a chair requires grouping shared features. Problem solving requires defining goals, available information, and permissible actions. Familiar solutions may help or obstruct new ones. (Spielman et al., 2020, §7.3 [R1](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/7-3-problem-solving))

Chapter question: How does reusing experience contribute to problem solving and new outputs?

### 2 Starting points in established research

#### 2.1 Forming categories and concepts

Concepts allow objects to be treated through common properties or relationships. Similarity to representative examples, remembered individual instances, and explicit rules may all participate. Research compares classification and judgment times for unfamiliar objects. (Spielman et al., 2020, §7.1 [R1](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/7-1-what-is-cognition))

#### 2.2 Problem representation changes solutions

How goals and permissible actions are represented changes the search space. Drawing relationships or externally recording intermediate results can change the conditions for handling a problem. (Spielman et al., 2020, §7.3 [R1](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/7-3-problem-solving))

#### 2.3 Analogy and reuse of experience

Problem-solving research distinguishes circumstances in which prior knowledge and familiar solutions help or hinder solving a new problem. (Spielman et al., 2020, §7.3 [R1](https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/7-3-problem-solving))

### 4 WRRA-specific transformation

Transformation specification: search and combine available relationships under constraints.

Concepts and knowledge are currently usable relational structures; problem solving connects them with present input under constraints. Minimal computation does not simply mean stopping thought quickly. It evaluates reduced unnecessary search while preserving the distinctions the task requires.

#### 4.1 Forming categories and concepts — WRRA interpretation

WRRA describes the retained relationships allowing a new input to enter a category. Identical classifications do not establish identical internal representations.

#### 4.2 Problem representation changes solutions — WRRA interpretation

WRRA distinguishes this change from increased internal individual ability. A combined person–recording-tool boundary allows evaluation of where information was maintained and which costs fell.

#### 4.3 Analogy and reuse of experience — WRRA interpretation

Relationships from earlier problems can be specified as presently usable residue. Tracing the connections actually contributing to solutions, while including superficially similar problems requiring different solutions, is this book's proposed study design.

### 5 Outputs and execution record

The current output is an explanatory specification of phenomena, variables, and validation paths. Planned outputs: solution rates, search costs, and evaluations of products.

### 6 Falsification conditions and interpretive limits

Falsification condition: limit general-creativity claims if success holds only by changing evaluation criteria.

Creative products require criteria for contextual appropriateness as well as novelty. Many answers differ from good answers. A WRRA model records memory, relationships, and search costs; outputs are assessed with separate criteria. Whether reuse of familiar connections and formation of new ones can actually be distinguished must also be checked.

#### 6.1 Evaluating creative outputs

Number, diversity, novelty, and appropriateness are different indicators. Evaluators and criteria should be disclosed and agreement examined. Even if WRRA structures generate new combinations, their value is judged by the relevant field's standards. Connecting computation and evaluation is needed to investigate when low cost and high creativity coincide.
